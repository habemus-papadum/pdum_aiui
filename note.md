Your two thoughts are actually one thought at different resolutions, and the bridge is Solomonoff induction: the ideal predictor is a Bayesian mixture over all programs, weighted by simplicity. That is literally an ensemble over simulations. Practical ensembling is the finite, computable shadow of it — you ensemble over model classes exactly when the data hasn't collapsed the posterior over "which program generated this." Low SNR and small effective sample size mean a diffuse posterior over program classes, so you hedge; internet-scale text means a concentrated posterior, so you commit all your capacity to one model. Idea 1 falls out of idea 2 plus Bayes.

Now the critiques. First, "high SNR" is the wrong description of text. Next-token prediction has large irreducible conditional entropy — loss plateaus far above zero. What's actually different is structure-to-noise times samples-per-unit-of-structure. Define signal as the variance (or mutual information) of the estimable conditional E[y|x]; text has enormous extractable structure and effectively unbounded samples, so estimation variance → 0 and you become approximation/capacity limited — scaling one model is compute-optimal. Markets have structure measured in basis points against volatility, with small and non-stationary effective n, so estimation variance is comparable to the signal itself — and ensembling, shrinkage, and equal weights are all variance-reduction moves. Equal-weight ensembles winning is the 1/N portfolio result transported to model space: when combination weights can't be estimated, don't estimate them.

Second, the RNG paradox dissolves once you split description length into structure plus noise — Kolmogorov sophistication, or two-part MDL. True random bits have maximal K but zero sophistication, and they're actually trivial to model distributionally: "predict uniform" hits the entropy floor immediately. What's impossible is pointwise prediction. So "hard to model" conflates a high noise floor with distance-to-floor. Learning difficulty ≈ sophistication (how much structure exists) × computational depth (how hard it is to extract). And compute matters independently of information: a PRNG's output has tiny K but is unlearnable by any efficient algorithm — the Kearns–Valiant cryptographic hardness results. So your simulation taxonomy needs resource bounds: not "how long is the program" but "how much structure can a poly-time learner extract per sample."

Third, the manifold-metric intuition is right but aimed at the wrong space. Three geometries get conflated: the data manifold, the smoothness of the target function on that manifold, and the loss landscape in parameter space — where gradient descent actually runs. The usable formalization of "how differentiable is the data" is spectral decay of the target in the eigenbasis of the Laplace–Beltrami or NTK operator on the data distribution. Spectral bias says GD learns eigendirections at rates proportional to eigenvalues: smooth-on-manifold targets learn fast; parity, crypto, and white-noise labels have flat spectra and never learn. Sample complexity then runs through intrinsic dimension and reach (Fefferman's manifold-hypothesis testing, Niyogi–Smale–Weinberger), with nonparametric rates like n^(−s/(2s+d)). And notably, where the geometry is singular — data concentrated on a low-dim manifold, or discrete tokens — we manufacture smoothness: diffusion models anneal noise precisely to make the score learnable. Smoothness is partly imposed, not discovered.

Chaos is the instructive middle case: a short program, a nice attractor manifold, a smooth flow map — short-horizon prediction is easy, long-horizon pointwise prediction is impossible because Lyapunov growth makes effective SNR decay exponentially in horizon, yet invariant statistics stay learnable forever. Learnability is indexed by functional and horizon, not a scalar property of a dataset — which is exactly the term-structure-of-predictability phenomenon in returns.

So the synthesis: achievable loss = entropy floor (the program's random bits) + approximation error (sophistication vs. capacity; architecture as a prior matched to the generating program — locality/symmetry → convnets and GNNs, compositional grammar → attention) + estimation error (samples vs. structure) + optimization error (spectral alignment). Your ensembling claim lives entirely in the estimation term; your simulation picture supplies the other three. One actionable corollary: held-out disagreement among heterogeneous models is an estimator of how diffuse the program posterior is — a direct diagnostic for when ensembling should pay.

Fair — I compressed about a semester into six paragraphs. Let me rebuild it properly: first a glossary where each concept gets a definition, a motivation, and an analogy, ordered so each builds on the last; then the laws that fall out of them.

The glossary
1. Pointwise vs. distributional prediction. Pointwise: given the input, predict the actual next value. Distributional: predict the probability distribution of the next value. A fair die makes the difference vivid — pointwise prediction is impossible, while the distributional model is one sentence: "uniform on 1–6." A casino has zero pointwise ability and a perfect distributional model, and it prints money. Every "how hard is this data" question is ill-posed until you fix which of these two games you're playing. LLMs play the distributional game (they output token probabilities); so does trading — you only need an edge in the distribution.

2. The noise floor. The randomness the data-generating program injects that no observer could remove — the literal coin flips inside the simulator. The best achievable loss equals this floor, not zero. Note that text has a high floor: "my favorite color is ___" is irreducibly uncertain, and LLM loss plateaus far above zero. So "text is high SNR" was the wrong frame — what makes text special lives elsewhere.

3. Signal (structure). The part of the output that is a genuine function of observable inputs — the variance of the conditional mean E[y|x], or the mutual information between input and output. Crucially, it's relative to an information set: what's noise given prices alone may be signal given order-flow data. In markets the structure is basis points sitting against percent-scale volatility; in language, the structure (grammar, facts, style, reasoning patterns) is enormous even though the floor is also high.

4. Effective sample size. How many independent looks at the structure you actually get. Non-stationarity is the killer: if the simulator's parameters drift, old samples describe a different program, so effective n stops growing with history length no matter how much data you buy. Markets are structure-poor and effective-sample-poor; the human-language generator is huge but comparatively stationary, and the web gives you astronomically many samples of it. The strategically decisive quantity is not SNR alone but structure per effective sample.

5. Kolmogorov complexity. The length of the shortest program that outputs your data — the ultimate zip file. This is the honest version of your "how complicated is the generating simulation" question, made language-independent.

6. Sophistication (two-part codes). Split that shortest description into two parts: the model part and the residual noise part. For a transcript of die rolls, the model part is one line ("iid uniform") and the residual is the raw rolls — total description huge, model part tiny. Sophistication = the size of the model part alone, i.e., the amount of genuine structure. This dissolves your RNG paradox: the RNG is maximally complex on the pointwise test and minimally complex on the distributional test. It only seemed both "simplest" and "hardest" because two different questions were being graded as one.

7. Computational depth. Information being present is not the same as being extractable with feasible compute. A pseudorandom generator is a tiny program — small Kolmogorov complexity, a short exact model exists — yet learning it from samples is cryptographically hard: no efficient algorithm beats uniform guessing (this is the classical Kearns–Valiant line of results, built on pseudorandom functions). Analogy: a locked safe containing a one-line note. So your taxonomy of simulations needs a third axis beyond program length and injected noise: compute required to extract the structure.

8. The four-term decomposition. This is the master equation:

loss = noise floor + approximation error + estimation error + optimization error

Approximation: your model family can't express the truth (fitting a sine with straight lines).
Estimation: finite noisy samples can't pin down which family member is right — refit on resampled data and the answer wobbles. This is "variance."
Optimization: the right function is in the family, but gradient descent can't find it.
Classical statistics is the middle two (bias–variance); deep learning forces you to take the fourth seriously. Diagnosing which term dominates is the whole strategic game.

9. Ensembling = variance reduction. Average M models with roughly independent errors and the estimation term shrinks like 1/M (less in practice, since errors correlate). Galton's ox: ~800 fairground guesses of an ox's weight, median within about 1% of truth — pure variance cancellation. Ensembling cannot lower the floor and barely touches approximation — unless the models are of different types, which is why your "different types" clause does double duty: it decorrelates errors and widens the union of families. The finance version is the 1/N result: equal-weight portfolios beat mean-variance-optimized ones out of sample because estimating the weights costs more than the optimization gains. Same theorem, different asset class: when combination weights can't be estimated, don't estimate them.

10. Solomonoff induction — the unifier. The ideal predictor runs every computable program as a candidate simulator, weights each by 2^(−length) (a simplicity prior), reweights by Bayes as data arrives, and predicts with the whole mixture. It's provably near-optimal and uncomputable — a north star, not a tool. Now the bridge between your two ideas: practical ensembling is a finite, affordable version of this committee of simulators. Scarce, noisy data leaves the posterior over program-classes diffuse — many kinds of simulator remain live — so you keep the committee. Internet-scale data collapses the posterior onto a narrow class, the committee becomes redundant, and the right move is pouring all capacity into one model of the winning class. Your idea 1 is the diffuse-posterior corollary of your idea 2.

11. The three geometries. Your manifold intuition was pointing at three different spaces that must not be conflated:

The data manifold — where x lives. Natural images form a thin curved sheet inside million-dimensional pixel space. Sample complexity rides the intrinsic dimension d: nonparametric error shrinks like n^(−s/(2s+d)), so smoothness s helps and intrinsic dimension hurts exponentially — the curse of dimensionality, priced at intrinsic rather than ambient dimension.
The target function on the manifold — how fast labels vary as you walk along the sheet. This is what your "smoothness of the metric" was reaching for.
The loss landscape in parameter space — where SGD actually hikes. A different world entirely; the data geometry can be lovely while this is nasty, and vice versa.
Analogy: the terrain, the temperature field painted on the terrain, and the mountain range some hiker climbs in another country.

12. Spectral bias. The precise cash-out of "can gradient descent work here." Expand the target function in the eigenbasis of a smoothness operator on the data (the graph Laplacian, or the network's NTK). Gradient descent learns each component at a rate proportional to its eigenvalue — coarse structure first, fine detail later, like a progressive JPEG sharpening. Smooth targets concentrate their energy in a few large-eigenvalue components: fast. Parity functions or white-noise labels smear energy flat across exponentially many components: gradient descent stalls essentially forever. "How differentiable is the data" becomes "how fast does the target's spectrum decay."

13. Manufactured smoothness. When the geometry is hostile — data concentrated on a razor-thin manifold, or discrete tokens with no geometry at all — we engineer smoothness rather than find it. Embeddings give tokens a continuous space. Diffusion models deliberately blur the data distribution with Gaussian noise at many scales so that the score (gradient of log-density) exists and is learnable everywhere, then anneal the blur away. Sanding a jagged surface so the ball can roll, then gradually restoring the detail. Note the lovely inversion of your framing: we add noise on purpose to make learning possible.

14. Horizon. A chaotic system — double pendulum, weather — is a short program with smooth dynamics and a nice attractor, yet a tiny state error grows like e^(λt), so pointwise SNR decays exponentially in the prediction horizon: weather forecasting dies at roughly two weeks. Meanwhile the attractor's statistics — climate — remain learnable forever. Learnability is therefore indexed by (dataset, functional, horizon), never by the dataset alone. Markets rhyme: the term structure of predictability, where seconds-scale microstructure and quarterly fundamental signals are different functionals of the same simulator with wildly different SNRs.

The laws
Floor law. No model beats the simulator's injected randomness; always ask "pointwise or distributional?" before asking "hard or easy?"
Decomposition law. Excess loss = approximation + estimation + optimization. Every famous technique attacks exactly one term; diagnose the dominant term before choosing weapons.
Matching law. An architecture is a prior over programs, and it pays exactly when its structure matches the generator's — convnets ↔ locality and translation symmetry, GNNs ↔ relational structure, attention ↔ compositional long-range dependency. No architecture wins across all simulators (no free lunch).
Ensembling law. Ensemble across model types when estimation error is comparable to signal — i.e., when the posterior over program-classes is diffuse. Commit everything to one scaled model when data has collapsed that posterior and you're approximation-limited.
Geometry law. Sample complexity rides intrinsic dimension and target smoothness; gradient descent extracts the spectrum top-down; when the geometry is bad, engineer it — embed, featurize, add annealed noise.
Compute law. Structure that no feasible algorithm can extract is noise in practice. "Learnable" is always relative to a compute budget.
Horizon law. Pointwise predictability decays with horizon at a rate set by the dynamics; distributional statistics can outlive trajectories indefinitely.
Back to your two ideas
Your second idea, properly formalized, is Solomonoff induction plus three refinements: measure the simulation by sophistication rather than raw program length (which resolves your RNG puzzle — that was a pointwise/distributional confusion), add a compute axis (PRNGs), and replace "smoothness of the metric" with spectral decay of the target on the data manifold. Your first idea then stops being a separate heuristic: ensembling-in-low-SNR is just what the ideal simulator-mixture reduces to when data hasn't collapsed the posterior. And there's a deployable diagnostic hiding in that: out-of-sample disagreement among heterogeneous models is an estimator of how diffuse your posterior still is — a live gauge for when to hedge across model classes versus when to scale one.
