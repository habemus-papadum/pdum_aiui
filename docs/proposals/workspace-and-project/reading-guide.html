A · levels of detail and focus

Does size pick detail, and what is a loupe?

Your weave example wants a continuous magnifier, not three rungs. Two of these are exactly that: a formula that assigns detail by interest, and a lens you drag over the content. Both could be a property of a station rather than of the surface.

Generalized Fisheye Views

George Furnas · Bell Labs · CHI 1986 · 8 pages · then the 2006 follow-up if it lands

The degree-of-interest function: a priori importance minus distance from the focus, shown above a threshold. It is the one-line answer to "which station gets detail", and it works on trees, calendars and code alike.

Look for the DOI formula and its two inputs, the threshold as a budget, and the code and calendar examples. In the follow-up, the distinction between what to show and how to show it.

Toolglass and Magic Lenses: the See-Through Interface

Bier, Stone, Pier, Buxton, DeRose · Xerox PARC · SIGGRAPH 1993 · 8 pages · two films

A transparent sheet between you and the document. Lenses on it re-render whatever is beneath them: magnified, filtered, wireframed, annotated. The loupe you described for weaves, with the twist that lenses compose when stacked.

Look for lenses as filters rather than zoom, the composition of two lenses, the two-handed input, and how the sheet avoids modes.

Pad++ and the zooming interface

Bederson and Hollan · UIST 1994 · after Perlin and Fox's Pad, 1993 · 10 pages · a three-minute film

An infinite surface where an object draws itself differently at each scale. That is the generated icon: the object owns its renderings per scale. It is also a warning about what an unstructured infinite canvas costs.

Look for semantic zoom on the calendar, portals (a view into another part of the surface, which is a station), and what they say about getting lost.

Engelbart's 1968 demo: viewspecs

Doug Engelbart and SRI's Augmentation Research Center · December 1968 · highlights 30 min, full 100 min

The outline on screen is viewed through a few switches: how many levels deep, how many lines per statement, with or without the numbering. Level of detail as a property of the view, set by a tiny vocabulary that works typed or spoken. Nothing in the content changes.

Look for the moments he collapses the list to first lines or to one level, the shared screen with Menlo Park, and how little vocabulary it takes.

B · views, rooms, layout rules

The same stations in several arrangements

Three different answers to how one set of things lives in many layouts: a window with a placement per room, a screen that is a tree of areas, and a layout that is a pure function.

Rooms: multiple virtual workspaces

Henderson and Card · Xerox PARC · ACM Transactions on Graphics 1986 · 33 pages, or the six-page technical description

A window can be in several rooms, with its own size and place in each. Doors lead between rooms. There is an Overview room that shows every room small. This is your workflow of views, built in 1986 from a study of how people thrash between windows.

Look for placements (one window, many positions), the Overview, Baggage (what travels with you through a door), and the task analysis at the start.

readtechnical description, scanned PDF readthe paper, ACM record readDigiBarn's screenshots 20 min · 90 min for the paper

Blender's screen model: workspaces, areas, editors

Blender Foundation · the manual, and the official Fundamentals series

A window is a tree of non-overlapping areas; any area can show any editor over the same file; a workspace is a tab of areas for one task. Area, editor, workspace, file map onto slot, kind, view, project. Splitting is the only layout operation there is.

Look for how a workspace tab remembers its layout, how an area changes editor type without moving, and what is per-workspace versus per-file. Play: install it, split an area, change its editor, switch tabs.

xmonad: layout as a pure function

Janssen, Stewart, Creighton · 2007 · a guided tour and one module's documentation

A layout takes an ordered list of windows and a rectangle and returns rectangles. Tall is a master area plus a stack; Mirror flips it; Full is one window; Choose lets you cycle. Hero plus rail and Fibonacci are two values of this one type, which is what a cell wants to compute.

Look for the master area, the ordering of windows as the only state, and the stated principle that the result of any action is predictable.

C · variations, sweeps, spikes

Where a variation lives, and whether a current pick exists

Four working answers. Sketchpad: masters and instances. Onshape: a table on the part plus a history graph. Houdini: an overlay of deltas over one base, so "current" is the empty overlay. Final Cut: a slot with a pick.

Sketchpad: a man-machine graphical communication system

Ivan Sutherland · MIT Lincoln Lab · 1963 · the film, and the thesis reprinted by Cambridge in 2003 with a new preface

A master drawing and its instances: change the master, every instance follows. Constraints between parts that relax toward a solution. The oldest answer to variation, and the data model behind every CAD system since.

Look for instances and subpictures in the film, then the chapters on the ring structure and on constraint satisfaction. Blackwell and Rodden's preface says what to skip.

watchthe 1963 film, 17 min readthe thesis, PDF watchSutherland's 1994 talk, CHM catalog 17 min · 2 h for the thesis

Onshape: configurations, versions, branches

PTC Onshape · the help pages and a one-hour webinar · a free account to play with

A part carries a table of named configurations over its parameters, which is a variation set on the node. The document itself has versions, branches and merges, so history belongs to the design and not to a file system. Both at once, in one product you can try tonight.

Look for the configuration table (rows are options, columns are parameters), what a branch is a pointer to, and the three merge choices. Play: make a part with two configurations, branch, change one, merge.

Houdini: takes, and wedging with TOPs

SideFX · the manual, and the Intro to PDG video series · Houdini Apprentice is free

A take is a layered set of parameter changes over the parent take; nothing is copied. A Wedge node generates one work item per variation of the attributes you name, and stacked wedges give the cross product. This is a variation as an overlay and a sweep as a product, which is closest to your "there may be no current".

Look for inheritance between takes, the auto-take mode, how a wedge attribute is referenced from a parameter, and what the results look like side by side.

Apple's answer: auditions and take folders

Final Cut Pro and Logic Pro · two support pages · both on your Mac if you have them

An audition is one slot in the timeline holding several alternates with one pick; swapping the pick disturbs nothing around it. A take folder holds the recorded takes and a comp is assembled from the best parts, then flattened. The variation set as a slot with a pick, stored in the document, and comping as a merge.

Look for what happens to the timeline when the pick changes length, and what flattening a comp keeps and throws away.

D · history, concurrent editing, forks

What git gives for free, and the eventual-consistency option

The deferred conflict question has three living answers: documents that merge by construction, last-writer-wins per property, and history as an editable program.

Ink and Switch: local-first, Upwelling, Patchwork

Kleppmann, Wiggins, van Hardenberg, McGranaghan · 2019 · then the lab's 2023 and 2024 essays

The essay sets out seven ideals for software whose data lives with you and merges without a server deciding. Upwelling gives writers private drafts that merge into a shared history. Patchwork is the lab's own version-controlled, branching document system, which they now work in. The eventual-consistency branch of your deferred question, worked out for documents.

Look for the seven ideals, Upwelling's layers and what a merge looks like to an editor, and Patchwork's branches with comments and history kept.

How Figma's multiplayer technology works

Evan Wallace · Figma · October 2019 · one post, and the branching guide

Two edits conflict only if they touch the same property of the same object, and then the last one wins. They looked at operational transforms and CRDTs and chose the simplest thing that holds. Branching came later as review-and-merge on top. The cheap option, argued honestly.

Look for why they rejected the heavier machinery, the undo rule (undo, copy, redo must leave the document unchanged), and how the server orders edits.

readthe post docsguide to branching 30 min

Fusion 360's parametric timeline

Autodesk · one help page · a personal license is free

The history of a part is a list of features you can scrub, edit in the middle, suppress, and replay. History as an editable program of the design, which is a different thing from a file's history and close to your lifecycle record.

Look for what happens to later features when you edit an early one, and the choice between capturing history and direct modeling. Play: sketch, extrude, fillet, then drag the marker back and change the sketch.

E · the document as the thing

Typed objects, generic verbs, and what stays addressable after it is drawn

The agent-friendly primitive you asked for was designed in 1981: a handful of universal commands over typed objects with property sheets. Two live-object systems and one Lisp machine show what happens when everything on screen stays an object.

Designing the Star User Interface

Smith, Irby, Kimball, Verplank, Harslem · Xerox · BYTE, April 1982 · 40 magazine pages · two films from 1982

A document-centric desktop with a small set of generic commands (move, copy, delete, show properties, again), property sheets instead of per-object menus, and progressive disclosure as a stated policy. The verb set transfers to an agent verbatim.

Look for the list of principles near the start, the universal commands, what they say about modes and about "seeing and pointing versus remembering and typing".

Self and Morphic; Boxer

Ungar and Smith, Sun Labs, 1995 film and the 2007 history paper  ·  diSessa and Abelson, Boxer, CACM 1986

In Self every object on screen is live: open it, drag a halo, change a slot, and the program changes. Boxer makes a box both data and display, and a port shows the same box live somewhere else, which is a station as a port into a node. Lively Kernel is Morphic running in a browser tab today.

Look for the outliner opening an object in place in the Self film, the morphic halo, and Boxer's ports and its naive-realism principle (what you see is all there is).

Genera's Dynamic Windows and presentations

Symbolics · 1986 onward · a period film, a 1990 manual, and a 2012 talk

Anything the system prints is a presentation of a typed object. A command that wants a pathname lets you click any pathname on screen, however long ago it was printed. Our stamps, generalized: what is drawn stays typed and addressable, and the tool surface falls out of it.

Look for clicking on previously printed output as an argument, the mouse documentation line at the bottom, and the chapter on presentations in the Concepts manual.

F · the lifecycle and the roles

Phases that gate strictness, and where the review point sits

None of these is software. Each is a profession that already runs the lifecycle you described, with a name for every phase and a place where the lead looks at the work.

The architect's phases: SD, DD, CD

Standard practice in US architecture · two firm explainers

Schematic design fixes the idea at low precision, design development proves it works, construction documents make it buildable. Each phase has its own precision and deliverable, and drawings move forward by markup and resubmission. Your lifecycle, with lint level per phase and markup as the regression gesture, already in daily use.

Look for what is decided and what is deliberately left open in each phase, and how a redline round is run.

NASA's readiness levels and design reviews

NASA · the TRL table, and sections 3.5 and 3.6 of the Systems Engineering Handbook

Nine readiness levels with an exit criterion each, and a preliminary and a critical design review as gates between phases, with entrance and success criteria written down. Going back a level is expected and named. Gates as criteria rather than labels.

Look for the exit criteria column, and the PDR and CDR entrance and success criteria in the handbook's appendix on reviews.

The brigade de cuisine

Escoffier · the Savoy, 1890s · an explainer with Michel Roux Jr's walkthrough, and the book

Stations with their own cooks, a sous-chef running the floor, the chef at the pass inspecting every plate before it leaves, an expediter calling tickets in order. Your analogy; one page of it gives us the names: station, pass, ticket.

Look for what the pass actually checks, what goes back and to whom, and how the expediter sequences a table's plates.

Pixar in a Box: the pipeline

Pixar and Khan Academy · 2015 onward · short films per stage

Story, then storyboards, then animatics, layout, animation, lighting, rendering: each stage a different artifact at a different fidelity, on purpose, with dailies as the standing review. A fidelity ladder with a distinct thing per rung.

Look for what each stage refuses to decide, and how a change late in the pipeline is handled.

G · the oldest precursors

Pen first, and the drawing as the design data

Before the mouse and before Smalltalk. Three films and one manual, chosen because each one is the thing itself, not a history of it.

GRAIL on the RAND tablet

Ellis, Heafner, Sibley · RAND · 1968 · a 14-minute film, Alan Kay's demonstration of it, and a RAND paper

Flowcharts drawn with a pen on a tablet, recognized live into a running program; no keyboard. The pencil as the first input into a structured document, with recognition as the editing flow, twenty years before anyone shipped it.

Look for how a drawn box becomes a typed node, how an edit is made by drawing over, and the latency they lived with.

Alto SIL, and the Early History of Smalltalk

Thacker, Sproull, Bates · PARC · the SIL manual, 1981  ·  Alan Kay · HOPL 1993

SIL drew the logic diagrams for PARC's own machines, and companion programs read connectivity off the drawings and produced the wire lists the shop built from. The drawing was the design data and the interface to fabrication. Kay's history gives the stance behind all of it.

Look for the pipeline from drawing to wirelist in the manual's first pages, and in Kay's paper the sections on the Dynabook and on what an object is.

The Alternate Reality Kit

Randall Smith · Xerox PARC · CHI+GI 1987 technical video · 16 min

A physics world in Smalltalk where you hold an interactor and pick up a gravity slider or a message as a thing on the screen. A station as a live experiment with knobs, and the direct ancestor of Self and Morphic.

Look for the interactor, messages as draggable objects, and how changing a law changes the running world.

The Smalltalk-80 system browser

Adele Goldberg · the orange book, 1984 · and Pharo, where the browser is the first thing you see

Four panes, each narrowing the next: category, class, protocol, method. A view that is a fixed chain of panes, the opposite of free placement, and some workflows want exactly this.

Play: open Pharo, open a System Browser, and watch how a click in one pane rewrites the panes to its right. Look for in the book, the chapter on the browser and the one on the debugger.
